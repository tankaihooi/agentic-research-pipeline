# Production RAG Vector Databases in 2026: Pinecone, Qdrant, Weaviate, and pgvector

*Research briefing · 23 September 2026 · 32 cited sources · 122 verified findings · depth: standard*

## Executive summary

- Pinecone offers automatically scaling on-demand reads or provisioned Dedicated Read Nodes, with hosted embedding and reranking options, but DRN capacity requires manual shard and replica decisions [1, 2, 3].
- Qdrant supports filtered, hybrid, and multi-vector retrieval across managed, hybrid, and self-hosted deployments, while reported high-write-volume problems depend on configuration and hardware [4, 5].
- Weaviate combines vector and BM25 search with granular per-property lexical controls and compound Search Mode retrieval, whose additional model inferences trade latency for quality [6, 7, 8].
- pgvector keeps retrieval in PostgreSQL and searches exactly by default, but approximate indexes trade recall for speed and apply filters after the index scan, potentially returning fewer matches than requested [9, 10].
- Costs follow different meters—Pinecone serverless usage, Qdrant allocated cluster resources, and Weaviate stored dimensions and storage—while self-hosting shifts spending toward infrastructure, making direct monthly-price comparisons difficult [3, 11, 12, 13, 14].
- No neutral matched benchmark establishes an overall winner, so teams should test retrieval quality, filters, P95/P99 latency at realistic QPS, and plan costs and availability on their own corpus [13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

## Pinecone: managed retrieval and provisioned-read options

Pinecone offers two approaches to production read capacity: an on-demand tier that scales automatically without a customer-selected node count, and Dedicated Read Nodes (DRNs) with provisioned capacity [1, 2]. Pinecone says DRNs use a warm data path, have fixed hourly per-node pricing, and avoid cold starts; its documentation says DRN indexes are not subject to read-unit limits for query, fetch, and list operations [3, 18]. DRN shards and replicas must currently be configured manually, so choosing provisioned reads also means taking on explicit capacity decisions [1].

For retrieval, Pinecone supports dense and sparse indexes and offers hosted reranking models that can run within a query or as standalone operations [1, 3]. Its hosted embedding models can likewise be used during upserting and querying or invoked separately [3]. Pinecone’s full-text search supports BM25 text scoring, Lucene-style query-string scoring, dense-vector scoring, and sparse-vector scoring [19]. † Pinecone also supports namespaces within an index without per-tenant provisioning [1]. For data freshness, Pinecone says writes are acknowledged immediately and become queryable in seconds, with background compaction re-optimizing data over time [1].

Pinecone runs on AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud, and its Builder plan supports serverless indexes in all generally available regions across those clouds as of June 2026 [1, 19]. Bring Your Own Cloud (BYOC) is in **public preview** on all three clouds: it places the data plane in the customer’s cloud account while Pinecone continues to operate the system [1, 19]. Pinecone describes BYOC as a zero-access operating model that does not require SSH, VPN, or inbound access to customer infrastructure [19].

Pinecone’s broader managed offering includes Assistant, described by a third-party article as RAG-as-a-Service, and an Inference API for native embedding generation [2]. Pinecone says its Nexus offering combines a context compiler with a composable retriever, but Nexus and KnowQL are open for **early access**, rather than presented as generally available capabilities [18]. Managed deployment also has boundaries: an article notes that Pinecone’s proprietary algorithms cannot be customized or optimized by users [2]. According to pecollective.com, Pinecone Local is an in-memory development emulator whose data does not survive restarts and which is not designed for production traffic, scale, or durability [20].

## Qdrant: filtered, hybrid, and multi-vector retrieval

Qdrant documents retrieval that combines vector similarity with metadata constraints: in one application it describes, embeddings are indexed with rich metadata filters and retrieved by similarity or natural-language queries [21]. Qdrant also describes a gardening application that uses its hybrid retrieval to search a curated knowledge base [21]. These examples show the range of retrieval patterns Qdrant presents, but they are application descriptions rather than comparative evaluations [21].

Filtering is a particular focus of Qdrant’s account of its architecture: the company says its approach avoids a separate pre-filtering or post-filtering step while addressing an accuracy problem [4]. In another Qdrant-described application, retrieval uses payload-filtered HNSW traversal alongside positive and negative recommendation anchors and the Discovery API [21]. Qdrant also describes use of its Recommend API with negative examples and a datetime payload filter to retrieve a specified recovery window [21]. According to firecrawl.dev, Qdrant supports JSON-based filters with nested properties and multiple conditions, with a query planner that optimizes execution [5]. According to xenoss.io, engineers can fine-tune Qdrant’s custom filtering settings to reduce missed relevant results or disruption to the HNSW graph [22].

For compound retrieval, Qdrant describes multi-vector prefetch combined with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), and another application stores six named vectors under one point ID [21]. According to firecrawl.dev, the broader feature set includes native sparse vectors alongside dense embeddings, ColBERT-style multi-vector late-interaction reranking, RRF or DBSF fusion across prefetches, and Maximal Marginal Relevance for result diversification [5].

Deployment options include open-source software and a managed service, with managed Qdrant Cloud and a Hybrid Cloud offering for Azure, GCP, and AWS [5]. Qdrant can also be self-hosted with Docker or Kubernetes [5]. According to checkthat.ai, the core database is Apache 2.0 open source and uses the same API across self-hosted, managed, and hybrid modes [17]. According to xenoss.io, Qdrant offers multitenancy through named collections or tenants, with quota controls, sharding, and tenant-level lifecycle APIs [22].

For capacity planning, ranksquire.com estimates that binary quantization compresses vectors by 32× and puts one million 1,536-dimensional vectors at approximately 200 MB; this is an estimate, not a measured deployment footprint [11]. According to firecrawl.dev, some users report problems at high write volumes, depending on configuration and hardware [5].

## Weaviate: hybrid search, lexical controls, and compound retrieval

Weaviate combines semantic vector search with BM25 keyword search in its built-in hybrid search [6]. Weaviate describes Query Agent’s Search Mode as a compound retrieval system designed for accurate search [8]. Weaviate says it applies query expansion, query decomposition, schema introspection, and reranking; the description identifies the techniques, but does not by itself establish how much accuracy improves on a particular workload [8].

**Lexical controls are comparatively granular.** Users can select tokenization per text property rather than per collection [7]. Weaviate also provides a built-in `trigram` tokenizer for three-character n-grams and fuzzy or substring matching, plus opt-in Japanese, Chinese, and Korean tokenizers enabled through environment variables [7]. Text properties can use accent folding while exempting specified characters; stopwords can be configured through named collection-level presets [7]. Because stopwords remain indexed and are filtered at query time, Weaviate says a preset change applies to the next query without reindexing [7]. In v1.37, `POST /v1/tokenize` and a property-scoped tokenize endpoint separately expose indexed tokens and the query tokens scored after stopword filtering, giving teams a way to inspect lexical behavior [7]. In Weaviate Cloud’s Explorer, filters are available in every mode, while sorting is limited to Browse mode [23].

Weaviate’s surrounding integrations can shorten the path from retrieval to an answer, subject to the application’s chosen services [6, 22]. According to xenoss.io, version 1.30 introduced a native `generative` module that retrieves results, sends them to a configured LLM provider, and returns an answer in one API call [22]. According to navanathjadhav.medium.com, `text2vec_openai` calls OpenAI’s embedding API automatically when text is inserted; firecrawl.dev describes a modular architecture for changing embedding models, vectorizers, and rerankers without rebuilding the application [5, 6].

Deployment shifts the operational burden rather than removing it. Weaviate can be self-hosted in Docker or Kubernetes, with infrastructure costs as the stated expense [5]. According to ranksquire.com, Premium BYOC instead runs in the customer’s cloud but is managed by Weaviate, whereas self-hosted OSS is customer-managed [13]. An author at navanathjadhav.medium.com cautions that schema changes require careful migration and that a separately run Weaviate service needs its own container, backups, and monitoring [6]. Finally, a Weaviate community-forum proposal described `Boost.decay` for property-based decay and filter-based boosts, and a forum participant later said it had merged and was live; those forum statements should not be mistaken for a detailed availability or deployment specification [24].

## pgvector: PostgreSQL-native retrieval and filtering trade-offs

Keeping retrieval in PostgreSQL can help when a RAG application needs to join and filter search results against its application data in SQL; PostgreSQL’s hosting-provider listing describes Rivestack search as allowing those operations in the same query [9]. According to the pgvector README, search is exact by default and provides perfect recall, while adding an approximate-nearest-neighbor index trades some recall for speed [10]. This makes the choice of index—and the way a query filters its candidates—central to the retrieval trade-off [10].

HNSW offers a better speed–recall trade-off than IVFFlat, but takes longer to build and uses more memory; IVFFlat builds faster and uses less memory, with a weaker speed–recall trade-off [10]. According to pgvector’s changelog, version 0.6.0 added parallel HNSW index builds and reduced their memory use and write-ahead-log generation [25]. The changelog also records that version 0.8.2, released February 25, 2026, fixed a buffer overflow in parallel HNSW index builds [25].

Filtering needs particular attention with approximate indexes: according to the pgvector README, filters are applied *after* the index scan, so a selective condition can leave fewer matching results than requested [10]. Its example says that if a condition matches 10% of rows, an HNSW scan using the default `hnsw.ef_search` of 40 yields only four matching rows on average [10]. According to the changelog, version 0.8.0, released October 30, 2024, added iterative index scans and improved cost estimation for index selection when filtering [25]. The README says iterative scans can keep searching until enough results are found or the configured `hnsw.max_scan_tuples` or `ivfflat.max_probes` limit is reached [10]. This suggests testing filtered queries for both result counts and scan limits, rather than assuming an approximate index will return the requested number of matches [10].

Storage and maintenance remain PostgreSQL capacity-planning concerns: the pgvector README specifies `4 * dimensions + 8` bytes for each single-precision `vector`, before considering indexes and other data [10]. According to pgvector’s changelog, version 0.7.0, released April 29, 2024, added `halfvec`, `sparsevec` and indexing of `bit` values, along with HNSW indexing for L1 distance and binary-quantization, Hamming-distance and Jaccard-distance functions [25]. The README recommends scaling vertically with more memory, CPU and storage, or horizontally through replicas or sharding approaches such as Citus or PgDog [10]. A Medium production-decision article reports HNSW rebuilds exceeding six hours on some production datasets beyond 50–100 million chunks; that is a reported experience, not a general rebuild-time guarantee [16].

## Pricing at a glance: usage meters, provisioned capacity, and self-hosting

The four products expose different cost drivers: Pinecone meters serverless storage and operations; Qdrant Cloud charges for allocated cluster resources; Weaviate Cloud meters stored vector dimensions, object storage, and backups; and self-hosting shifts software charges toward infrastructure spending [3, 11, 12, 13]. According to tech-insider.org, these differences make direct monthly-price comparisons difficult, and its workload scenarios are directional estimates based on public prices, not vendor quotes or guaranteed invoices [14].

**Pinecone: usage meters within plan terms.** Pinecone’s serverless charges use read units, write units, storage, and egress; an index with no stored data or operations has no usage charges, while stored data remains billable even without operations [3]. The Builder plan is a flat $20 per month, includes 10 serverless indexes, 10 GB of organization storage, and 100 namespaces per index, and blocks usage beyond its limits rather than billing overages [3, 19]. Standard and Enterprise have monthly minimum usage commitments of $50 and $500, respectively [3]. Standard’s optional HIPAA add-on costs $190 per month; Pinecone says HIPAA compliance is included with Enterprise [19]. Separately, Pinecone Assistant’s Starter allowances reset monthly and include 500,000 chat input tokens, 300,000 chat output tokens, 500,000 context retrieval tokens, and 1,000 ingestion units [19]. A trantorinc.com page lists serverless storage at $0.33 per GB-month plus separate read and write charges, while SpendArk notes that separately metered reads can raise query-heavy costs [26, 27]. Pecollective.com estimates $300–$800 per month or more for at least 10 million vectors and over 100,000 daily queries, particularly with heavy writes or complex filtered queries; that is a workload estimate, not a plan price [20].

**Qdrant: provisioned resources or customer infrastructure.** Qdrant Managed Cloud bills hourly for resources, while Hybrid and Private Cloud pricing is custom or unpublished [17]. As of April 2026, a listed free Cloud tier offered 0.5 shared vCPU, 1 GB RAM, and 4 GB disk at $0 per month [11]. According to ranksquire.com, Standard charges for allocated cluster capacity rather than queries or writes [11]. That site estimates $30–$60 monthly for a 2 GB RAM, 1 vCPU, 20 GB disk cluster and $120–$200 for an 8 GB cluster; these are approximate configuration costs, not fixed plan fees [11]. According to lambdadb.ai, a different tested configuration cost $1,673.73 monthly regardless of utilization, illustrating why cluster size matters to the comparison [28]. Self-hosted Qdrant OSS has no Apache 2.0 license fee, while according to ranksquire.com, Hybrid Cloud runs on customer infrastructure with Qdrant-managed operations and custom pricing [11].

**Weaviate: dimensions, storage, and replication.** Weaviate Cloud’s model, updated in October 2025, bills stored vector dimensions, persistent object storage, and backup storage [12]. As of April 2026, Flex had a $45 monthly minimum and a rate of $0.01668 per million vector dimensions per month [13]. Ranksquire.com lists a 14-day Flex trial and says its 14-day Sandbox cannot be extended and that the cluster is gone when the trial expires [13]. For 5 million 1,536-dimensional vectors at replication factor two, ranksquire.com estimates approximately $312 monthly without Binary Quantization versus $64 with it, including estimated object and backup storage [13]. The same page says Binary Quantization reduces dimension billing by approximately 97%; the worked totals are illustrative rather than a universal Weaviate bill [13]. Self-hosted Weaviate OSS has no BSD-3 software license fee, but requires infrastructure spending; ranksquire.com cites a $96-per-month, 16 GB, 8-vCPU DigitalOcean Droplet as one self-hosting example, with its capacity estimates varying by quantization [12, 13].

**PostgreSQL hosting:** According to a postgresql.org hosting-provider listing, Rivestack offers fixed per-node pricing from $29 monthly without usage metering; this is a provider offer, not a general pgvector deployment cost [9].

## Performance claims and benchmarks: what can actually be compared?

There is no single leaderboard in these findings that puts all four options under matched conditions. Tech Insider reported in August 2026 that it found no neutral, matched comparison of Pinecone, Weaviate, and Qdrant; according to the same source, ann-benchmarks.com does not maintain a current three-way managed-service comparison, and Pinecone’s proprietary index cannot be tested outside its managed API [14]. † Tech Insider also characterizes Qdrant’s claims of leading throughput and latency in almost all scenarios in its own suite as vendor-run, directional evidence rather than an independent audit [14].

**Retrieval quality is not interchangeable with speed.** Weaviate reports that Search Mode improved mean Success @ 1 by 17% and Recall @ 5 by 11% over Hybrid Search across its tested benchmarks; on BRIGHT Biology, it reports nDCG @ 10 of 0.40 versus 0.13 [8]. Weaviate also claims gains over the best methods in the original BRIGHT paper of 13.4, 9.6, 2.6, 12.2, and 8.8 nDCG @ 10 points on Biology, Earth Science, Economics, Psychology, and Robotics, respectively, and says three trials produced fairly consistent results despite model stochasticity [8]. Weaviate says Search Mode’s additional model inferences trade some latency for quality and that Hybrid Search may better suit latency-sensitive uses [8]. One Weaviate forum user reported a much slower, specific case: hybrid searches across nearly 11 million objects with a cross-reference filter and vector averaged 30 seconds and sometimes reached two minutes [29]. Qdrant says its filtered-ANN datasets span synthetic and real-world data, with keyword-to-geospatial filters, and that it built a separate 10-billion-document benchmark with ground truth [4].

**Published throughput figures describe different tests:**

- According to Kalvium Labs, Qdrant-published tests on 1 million 768-dimensional vectors, HNSW, and approximately 99% recall put Qdrant at about 850 QPS and 8 ms p95, versus Weaviate at about 380 QPS and 18 ms p95 [30]. Kalvium Labs reports that its own production measurements on 500,000–1 million 1,536-dimensional vectors put pgvector HNSW at about 220 QPS/48 ms p95, HNSW with four parallel workers at 360 QPS/58 ms, IVFFlat at 90 QPS/70 ms, and Pinecone Serverless in us-east-1 at 340 QPS/28 ms [30]. Its stated baseline is a 1-million-vector, cosine-distance, 1,536-dimensional corpus unless noted; it says the Qdrant figures use Qdrant’s hardware and its own spot checks, rather than the production systems behind its pgvector and Pinecone figures [30]. Kalvium Labs also reports pgvector p95 of 80–140 ms at 5 million vectors, depending on `ef_search`, versus under 30 ms for pod-based Pinecone deployments [30].
- A comparison on identical AWS hardware reported pgvectorscale—not baseline pgvector—at 471.57 QPS versus Qdrant at 41.47 QPS at 99% recall, while Qdrant’s tail latency was 39–48% lower [17]. According to Tech Insider’s account of a third-party comparison, the approximately 41-versus-471-QPS result used 50 million vectors and a recall-focused test [14].
- According to LambdaDB’s May 19, 2026 benchmark, Qdrant Cloud recorded 33.54 ms median query latency, versus 78.44 ms for LambdaDB and 53.44 ms for its 16-partition configuration, and reached 391.59 QPS at 16 clients and 385.90 QPS at 32 [28]. LambdaDB specifies a preloaded 10-million-vector English subset of Cohere Wikipedia 2023-11 embeddings at 1,024 dimensions, queried for 30 seconds per concurrency level; its Qdrant Cloud configuration used three 8-core, 32-GB nodes with 240-GB disks, replication factor two, at $1,673.73 per month [28].
- According to Strapi, Pinecone achieved 74,000 QPS at 90% recall on filtered YFCC-10M in BigANN 2023; Strapi separately reports approximately 45 ms p50 and 96 ms p99 at sustained 600 QPS with 135 million vectors [2]. Pinecone says production Dedicated Read Node workloads see 77–97% cost reductions at scale; it separately claims Nexus and KnowQL produced task-completion rates above 90% and 30-times-faster time-to-completion [18].

These figures should not be ranked as though vector dimensions and collection sizes were held constant across tests [17, 28, 30]. According to Tech Insider, dataset shape, index tuning, network round trips, embedding batch size, `ef_construction`, and `ef_search` can materially change measured results; a Medium production guide likewise identifies filtering, scale, latency tolerance, and concurrency as determinants of what is “fastest” [14, 16]. According to Blck Alpaca, adding hybrid retrieval and a cross-encoder reranker can bring pipeline latency to 150–500 ms, with the reranker the latency-elastic component—another reason not to equate index latency with end-to-end RAG latency [31].

## Production choice: match retrieval quality, operations, and costs to the workload

The selection test is not which system leads a general benchmark, but which one meets the team’s retrieval and operating requirements on its own corpus. According to aiml.qa, teams should test on their actual data rather than rely on vendor benchmarks [15]. Its recommended measures are Precision@K, Recall@K, and MRR on a golden set, alongside P95/P99 query latency at production-realistic QPS [15].

**Make filters part of the test, not an afterthought.** A production decision guide on medium.com argues that filter performance is often more consequential than improving vector recall above approximately 95% [16]. A comparison page describes pgvector as filtering a scanned candidate set and Qdrant as applying filters during graph traversal, making Qdrant more precise for aggressive filters in that account [30]. Teams should therefore test filter performance alongside retrieval quality and P95/P99 query latency at production-realistic QPS [15, 16, 30].

**Match the operating model to the team.** According to trantorinc.com, Pinecone is a practical choice for a small team with limited operations capacity, fewer than roughly 50 million vectors, and a need to ship RAG quickly without managing infrastructure [26]. A comparison page lists Pinecone as HIPAA certified and specifies a 99.95% uptime SLA for Enterprise; teams with those requirements should check the applicable offering rather than assume they apply to every plan [32]. For some DACH projects below roughly 50 million vectors, blckalpaca.at instead frames managed Postgres with pgvector or pgvectorscale as the simpler operational and sovereignty choice: one database, backup arrangement, and GDPR DPA chain—not proof that Qdrant is slow [31].

Finally, test the cost and availability assumptions of the *intended* plan. According to checkthat.ai, Qdrant Managed Cloud’s free tier is a single node with 1 GB RAM, 4 GB disk, and 0.5 vCPU, without high availability [17]. According to ranksquire.com, Weaviate’s Flex plan has a $45/month minimum and a 99.5% SLA [13]. Those plan details should be considered alongside availability needs and corpus-specific quality and latency results [13, 15, 17].

## Methodology & limitations

- **Research questions:** 5 planned, plus 3 follow-up searches the Critic requested (1 gap loop; research stopped because: coverage met).
- **Sources read:** 33 pages, 15 of them on the official sites of the companies covered.
- **Verification:** 289 extracted findings were checked. Each quote was matched against its source text, each claim against its quote, and third-party claims against the other sources.
  - 122 verified (69 stated by the vendor about itself), 136 single-source (attributed in the text), 16 contradicted by another source (noted in the text).
  - 15 rejected and excluded: 1 quotes not found in their source, 14 claims their quote did not support, 0 failed cross-referencing. Most common reasons: overstates its quote (13); quote not found in source (80% best match) (1); unsupported (1).
- **Sentence audit:** 115 statements were checked against the findings they cite; 12 were flagged and revised. 2 still flagged after revision are marked †.
- **Limits:** web sources as retrieved on the date above; vendor pages describe their own products favourably, and pricing changes often. Treat single-source and contradicted claims as leads to confirm.
