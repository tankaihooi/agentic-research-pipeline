# EU AI Act obligations for general-purpose AI model providers: duties and compliance dates through 2027

*Research briefing · 23 September 2026 · 30 cited sources · 145 verified findings · depth: standard*

## Executive summary

- GPAI rules apply to providers of broadly capable models placed on the market, and systemic-risk classification can arise from high-impact capabilities, Commission designation, or a presumption triggered by training compute exceeding 10²⁵ FLOPs [1, 2, 3].
- All GPAI providers must maintain technical documentation, supply information to downstream providers, adopt a Union copyright-compliance policy, and publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content [4].
- Qualifying free-and-open-source models are exempt only from the technical-documentation and downstream-information duties; copyright and public-summary duties remain, and systemic-risk classification removes the exception entirely [4, 5].
- Providers of systemic-risk models must additionally conduct and document evaluations including adversarial testing, assess and mitigate Union-level risks, report serious incidents, and protect their models and physical infrastructure against cybersecurity threats [6, 7].
- Commission guidance is non-binding, the training-content template addresses a required public disclosure, and the voluntary Code of Practice can demonstrate compliance but does not confer a presumption of conformity [8, 9, 10].
- Relevant GPAI obligations apply to models placed on the EU market from 2 August 2025, while providers of models already on the market before that date have until 2 August 2027 to comply [1, 11].
- Commission enforcement powers and Article 101 fines apply from 2 August 2026, with breaches of GPAI-model obligations subject to fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher [11, 12, 13].

## Who is covered, and when does a model present systemic risk?

The GPAI rules concern providers of *general-purpose AI models*: models with significant generality that can competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks and be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications, regardless of how they are placed on the market [1]. The definition excludes models used for research, development or prototyping before they are placed on the market [1].

The Act separately defines a *downstream provider* as the provider of an AI system that integrates an AI model, whether it supplies that model itself through vertical integration or obtains it from another entity under a contractual relationship [14]. This distinction helps identify the model-provider role addressed by the GPAI rules and the system-provider role that may incorporate the model [1, 14].

A GPAI model falls into the additional systemic-risk regime if it has high-impact capabilities assessed using appropriate methodologies and technical tools, or if the European Commission designates it under the Annex XIII criteria [2, 3]. Cumulative training compute **exceeding 10²⁵ floating-point operations (FLOPs)** triggers a presumption that the model presents systemic risk [2, 6]. Article 55’s additional obligations apply to providers of GPAI models above that threshold or otherwise classified as presenting systemic risk under Article 51 [7].

Compute is not the only route to classification: the Commission can designate a model below the threshold based on criteria including its number of users, modalities, scientific or technical complexity, market reach, or capabilities equivalent to state-of-the-art GPAI models [6]. A separate account says the European AI Office may designate additional models as having systemic risk; it also lists reach and EU user numbers, economic or societal dependence, multimodal capabilities, access to exceptional resources or capabilities, and potential significant harms to public health, safety, security or fundamental rights [15]. According to the European Commission’s guidance, systemic risks include risks to fundamental rights and safety, as well as risks related to loss of control over the model [11].

## Baseline Article 53 duties for GPAI providers

Article 53(1) sets four baseline duties for providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models placed on the EU market: maintain technical documentation, supply information to downstream AI-system providers, adopt a copyright-compliance policy, and publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content [4].

**Technical documentation.** Providers must draw up and keep current documentation of the model’s training and testing process and evaluation results, containing at least the elements specified in Annex XI; they must make it available to the AI Office and national competent authorities on request [16, 17, 18]. The documentation covers the model’s description, intended tasks, architecture and parameter count, as well as provider contact details, version and release date, licence, input and output modalities, and external interactions [18, 19]. It also addresses integration requirements, design choices and assumptions, training methods, training, testing and validation data, data sources and curation, bias-detection measures, computational resources, training duration, and estimated or known energy consumption [18, 19].

**Information for downstream providers.** Providers must draw up, keep current and make available Annex XII-level information and documentation to AI-system providers intending to integrate the model [16, 17]. It must enable those providers to understand the model’s capabilities and limitations and meet their own AI Act obligations, including Chapter 2 duties if they build a high-risk system on the model [16, 19]. This information requirement is subject to the protection of intellectual property rights, confidential business information and trade secrets under Union and national law [17].

**Copyright policy.** Providers must put in place a policy to comply with Union copyright and related-rights law, particularly to identify and comply with rights reservations under Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790, including through state-of-the-art technologies [16, 17, 18]. The policy therefore addresses rights holders’ text-and-data-mining opt-outs; a compliance guide describes identifying and respecting them as a good-faith effort [16, 19].

**Public training-content summary.** Providers must publish a sufficiently detailed summary of content used to train the model, following the AI Office’s template [16, 17, 20, 21]. The summary is intended to give an overview of training data, identify main data collections and explain other sources [9]. According to cliffordchance.com, it covers training stages from pre-training through post-training but, in principle, not operational inputs such as retrieval-augmented-generation inputs [21]. The same source says the template calls for provider, model and training-data information; a list of datasets, scraped material and other sources; and data-processing information, including measures concerning rights reservations and illegal content [21]. It says providers should publish the summary on their official website and public distribution channels no later than EU market placement, updating it after further training where required [21]. GPAI providers must use the EU SEND platform to submit documents related to their obligations to the AI Office [8].

## The qualified open-source exception—and its limits

Article 53(2) offers qualifying free-and-open-source general-purpose AI (GPAI) model providers a **limited exception**, not a blanket exemption: it waives the Article 53(1)(a) technical-documentation duty and the Article 53(1)(b) duty to provide information to downstream providers integrating the model into AI systems [4, 5]. The Article 53(1)(c) copyright-policy duty and Article 53(1)(d) public summary of training content still apply [4, 5].

To qualify, the model must be released under a free and open-source licence permitting access, use, modification and distribution, and its **weights, architecture information and usage information** must be publicly available [4]. The licence may allow users to run, copy, distribute, study, change and improve the model while requiring attribution to the original provider and identical or comparable distribution terms [1]. According to orrick.com, the Commission’s GPAI provider guidelines interpret “usage” to mean that the original provider cannot invoke intellectual-property rights to restrict use of the model or charge for that use [22]. The same source reports that the guidelines nevertheless permit safety-oriented licence terms that reasonably restrict use in applications or domains where the model could pose significant risks to public interests, including health, security and safety [22].

**Systemic risk ends the Article 53(2) exception:** a model classified as posing systemic risk under Article 51 remains subject to the full Article 53(1) baseline duties as well as the additional Article 55 duties, even if it is released openly [4]. According to regumatrix.eu, the separate open-source exception from appointing an authorised representative for a non-EU GPAI provider likewise falls away when the model has systemic risk [5]. Regumatrix.eu also notes that open-source status does not relieve a downstream provider of its obligations under Articles 9–15 when it integrates the model into a high-risk AI system [5].

## Additional Article 55 duties for systemic-risk models

Article 55 adds four duties for providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models with systemic risk; it does not replace the baseline Article 53 obligations [4, 7]. The additional duties concern model evaluation, assessment and mitigation of Union-level systemic risks, serious-incident reporting, and cybersecurity [4].

**Evaluate the model.** Providers must conduct and document model evaluations using state-of-the-art protocols and tools, including adversarial testing [6]. The adversarial testing must be conducted and documented to identify and mitigate systemic risks [7].

**Assess and mitigate systemic risks.** Providers must assess possible systemic risks at Union level and mitigate them, identifying their sources and how they might emerge along the AI value chain [7]. The assessment encompasses risks that may stem from developing the model, placing it on the market, or using it [6]. According to complyjudge.com, these assessments must be ongoing rather than a one-time pre-deployment exercise, covering foreseeable misuse, changes in capability as a model is fine-tuned or updated, newly identified threat vectors, and interactions with downstream uses [15]. Complyjudge.com also says providers must retain assessment documentation and make it available to the AI Office on request [15].

**Handle serious incidents.** Providers must track and document serious incidents, then report relevant information about them and possible corrective measures without undue delay to the AI Office and, where appropriate, national competent authorities [6]. The duty thus includes keeping a record of incidents as well as reporting them and potential corrective measures [6, 7].

**Protect the model and infrastructure.** Providers must ensure an adequate level of cybersecurity protection for both the systemic-risk GPAI model and its physical infrastructure [6, 7]. Together, these duties add evaluation, risk management, incident handling, and cybersecurity requirements to the Article 53 obligation set [4, 7].

## Compliance tools: Commission guidance, template, and voluntary Code

Providers have several tools for explaining how they comply, but those tools carry different legal weight: the Commission’s guidance is interpretive, the training-content template addresses a specific public disclosure, and the voluntary Code of Practice offers a way to demonstrate compliance without creating a presumption of conformity [8, 9, 10].

**Guidance and template.** According to paulweiss.com, the Commission published non-binding Guidelines on the scope of GPAI model obligations on 18 July 2025 [23]. According to digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, the Guidelines reflect the Commission’s interpretation and will guide enforcement, but are not legally binding [8]. According to digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, the Commission published a template to help GPAI providers summarise the content used to train their models [9]. The template is intended to help providers make a summary of training content publicly available in line with the AI Act; the Commission also said it would help parties with legitimate interests, such as copyright holders, exercise their rights under Union law [9]. According to digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, the template outlines the required content for the public summary under Article 53(1)(d) [10].

**Code of Practice.** The Commission describes the GPAI Code of Practice as a voluntary tool, and the Commission and Member States have confirmed it as an adequate voluntary means for providers to demonstrate compliance [24, 25]. Its Transparency and Copyright chapters address all GPAI model providers, while its Safety and Security chapter addresses providers of models with systemic risk [26]. According to digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, the first two chapters give signatories further detail on complying with Article 53(1)(a), (b), and (c); the Code also gives signatories further detail on the Article 55 systemic-risk obligations [10].

Adhering to the Code can demonstrate compliance, but implementing or signing it does **not** confer a presumption of conformity; providers may demonstrate compliance by other means [10, 27]. Providers that choose not to follow a code must support compliance with other evidence, and those that neither adhere to an approved code nor comply with harmonised standards must demonstrate alternative adequate means to the AI Office [7, 19]. The Commission may subject non-signatories to enhanced scrutiny, including more information requests and access for model evaluations than signatories face [23]. Providers may rely on Article 56 codes of practice until harmonised standards are published; unlike Code adherence, compliance with published harmonised standards grants a presumption of conformity [7, 10].

## Compliance and enforcement timeline: August 2025 to August 2027

**2 August 2025 — obligations begin for new models.** The AI Act’s GPAI-provider obligations under Articles 53 and 55 apply from this date, and providers placing GPAI models on the market from then must comply with the obligations relevant to them immediately [1, 11, 28, 29]. The copyright and training-summary obligations in Articles 53(1)(c) and (d) are among the Article 53 obligations that apply from 2 August 2025 [1, 21]. According to paulweiss.com, providers first placing a GPAI model on the EU market from 2 August 2025 must publish a completed training-content Template when they place it on the market [23].

**2 August 2026 — enforcement powers apply.** According to digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, the Commission’s enforcement powers enter into application on this date; the AI Office’s enforcement powers over GPAI providers also became active then [11, 12]. From that date, the AI Office and Member State authorities are responsible for implementing, supervising and enforcing the AI Act [30]. For GPAI models, the AI Office can request technical documentation, evaluate models, require corrective measures and issue fines for non-compliance [30]. According to digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, it can also request access to models for evaluations and ask providers to take measures, including restricting a model’s public availability if necessary [13]. Article 101 EU-level fines for GPAI providers apply from 2 August 2026, and breaches of GPAI-model obligations may attract fines of **up to €15 million or 3% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher** [12, 13].

**2 August 2027 — deadline for pre-existing models.** According to digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu, providers of GPAI models already on the market before 2 August 2025 must comply with the relevant obligations by 2 August 2027 [11]. Other accounts likewise describe a transition period ending on that date and require providers to take the necessary steps toward compliance; one says providers must be able to show those steps during the transition [12, 28, 29]. According to paulweiss.com, providers of those pre-existing models also have until 2 August 2027 to complete and publish their training-content Templates [23].
